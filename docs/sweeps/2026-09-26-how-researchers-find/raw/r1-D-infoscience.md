## Findings
F1. In a large review of complex evidence, the pre-planned protocol (15 databases plus hand-searching 32 journals) found 30% of the 495 cited sources. Chasing references found 51%, and what the team knew or whom they knew found 24%. Yield per hour: databases, 1 useful paper per 40 min; hand-search, 1 per 9 h; forward citation tracking, 1 per 15 min. 5 sources came by chance, one "in a coffee queue". The team browsed widely before running the protocol.
    who:      Greenhalgh & Peacock, UCL; 6 researchers; nothing to sell (Europe PMC cites 979)
    evidence: "Only 30% of sources were obtained from the protocol defined at the outset of the study (that is, from the database and hand searches). Fifty one per cent were identified by “snowballing”" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1283190/ (2026-09-26; READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q1, Q3, Q6, C1, C2
    limit:    One review of mixed policy evidence. The same paper says that for Cochrane therapy reviews "Searching 26 further databases identified only an extra 2.4% of trials".

F2. Checking reference lists adds studies no other search found, but how many varies roughly 17-fold across studies. Doing it in repeated rounds is rare.
    who:      Horsley et al., Cochrane Methodology Review (12 studies); Hirt et al. 2023 scoping review (47 studies)
    evidence: "of relevant studies identified through checking reference lists ranged from 2.5% to 42.7%" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7388740/ (READ-FULL). Hirt: "Application of multiple citation indexes in parallel, co-citing or co-cited references, CT iterations, or software tools was rare." — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37042216/ (READ-PARTIAL, abstract)
    bears on: Q1, C1
    limit:    9 of the 12 studies are case reports. Yield depends on how good the database search was.

F3. Snowballing starts from a seed set that must span separate clusters of papers. It alternates backward and forward chaining one round at a time and stops when a round finds nothing new. Then it contacts the most active authors and restarts. Included papers per round fell from 3/13 to 7/25, then 1/223, then 0/33. It found the same studies as the original review, which used database searches.
    who:      Claes Wohlin, Blekinge Inst. of Technology, software engineering (EASE 2014)
    evidence: "Once no new papers are found in the iterations using both backward and forward snowballing, the loop is ended. To complement the snowballing, it is recommended to contact the authors of included papers" — https://www.wohlin.eu/ease14.pdf (READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q1, Q4, Q6, C1, C2
    limit:    One replication by one author on a topic of about 10 papers. He admits he remembered the original found "10-19 papers". If the seed set misses an "independent cluster", chaining never reaches it.

F4. Across 58 reviews (1,746 included references), 16% of references were found in only one database. Embase alone recalled 85.9%; four databases together recalled 98.3%. Papers read per inclusion rose from 57 to 73. The authors estimate 60% of published reviews miss more than 5% of relevant references.
    who:      Bramer et al., Erasmus MC library (Europe PMC cites 1,237)
    evidence: "Sixteen percent of the included references (291 articles) were only found in a single database" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5718002/ (READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q3, Q6
    limit:    One expert searcher, biomedicine only. The paper contradicts itself: 291 (16%) in the abstract, 292 (17%) in the results.

F5. How US science faculty learned of the last article they read, 1977 → 2005: browsing 58.5% → 33.9%; automated search 0.7% → 23.1%; citations 6.7% → 14.9%; people 17.7% → 18.5%. The absolute number of articles found by browsing held steady (88 → 95 a year).
    who:      Tenopir (UTK) & King (UNC); the same "last article read" questionnaire since 1977
    evidence: "Ironically, reading patterns have broadened with electronic journals at the same time citing patterns have narrowed." — https://doi.org/10.1045/november2008-tenopir (READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q1, C4
    limit:    US university science faculty only. Samples range from n=2,350 (1977) to n=70 (1993) to n=884 (2005), and each answer is one recalled incident.

F6. Berrypicking: the query itself changes as you search, and results are collected piece by piece rather than as one final set. The survey Bates cites: over 87% of social-science faculty rarely or never got references from abstracting services, and 69% relied on footnote chasing.
    who:      Marcia Bates, UCLA (1989, author's own copy)
    evidence: "Each new piece of information they encounter gives them new ideas and directions to follow and, consequently, a new conception of the query." — https://pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/bates/berrypicking.html (READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q1, Q4, C1
    limit:    A conceptual paper; its evidence is other people's studies, before the web.

F7. Ellis described six activities: starting, chaining, browsing, differentiating, monitoring, extracting. His 1993 comparison found that "fundamental differences in information seeking behaviour could not be determined" between physicists/chemists and social scientists. A re-test with 60 social scientists added networking, verifying (cross-checking biased sources with colleagues and officials) and information managing.
    who:      David Ellis (Sheffield) 1989; Ellis, Cox & Hall 1993; Meho & Tibbo 2003 (60 faculty in 14 countries)
    evidence: "I send members of this network anything I come across and bother them to send me stuff when I need it" — http://eprints.rclis.org/8364/1/meho-tibbo.pdf (READ-FULL); Ellis abstracts via https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1108/eb026843 , https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1108/eb026919 (READ-PARTIAL)
    bears on: Q1, Q2, Q5, Q7, C6
    limit:    Stopping was not studied: "Although it was not discussed in this study, an ending stage was assumed". The topic (stateless nations) makes checking sources unusually important.

F8. Kuhlthau's search process runs from exploring a general topic, to forming a focus, to collecting focused material. Uncertainty rises during exploration, and the closing signal is redundancy. Detailed notes taken early "may thwart the process by seeking premature closure."
    who:      Carol Kuhlthau, Rutgers; 5 studies, including 147 high-school seniors and 385 library users at 21 sites
    evidence: "Actions involve a summary search in which decreasing relevance and increasing redundancy are noted in the information encountered." — https://wp.comminfo.rutgers.edu/ckuhlthau2/wp-content/uploads/sites/185/2016/01/InsidetheSearchProcess.pdf (READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q4, Q6, C2
    limit:    Mostly students on assigned topics. Only "50% making focused statements" at closure.

F9. Swanson connected two literatures that never cited each other. Fish oil changes blood (A→B), those blood changes would help Raynaud's (B→C), so fish oil may help Raynaud's (A→C). An independent trial followed (n=32): Raynaud's could no longer be induced in 5 of 11 fish-oil patients, against 1 of 9 on placebo. ARROWSMITH later automated the search for shared B-terms.
    who:      Don Swanson, Univ. of Chicago library school (1986 paper, OpenAlex cites 893); DiGiacomo et al. (trial)
    evidence: "Yet these two literatures had no articles in common and had never before been cited together" — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2403828/ (READ-PARTIAL, abstract); trial https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2536517/
    bears on: Q3, Q4, C3
    limit:    The trial found benefit in "primary, but not secondary" Raynaud's. Three worked examples; failed attempts are not counted.

F10. The papers most likely to be hits are mostly conventional pairings with a few unusual ones mixed in. Top-5% hit rate: 9.11 per 100 for that mix, against a background of 5. Novelty without a conventional base: 5.33; low on both: 2.05. Teams are 37.7% likelier than solo authors to add novel pairings.
    who:      Uzzi, Mukherjee, Stringer & Jones, Northwestern (17.9M Web of Science papers)
    evidence: "The highest-impact science is primarily grounded in exceptionally conventional combinations of prior work yet simultaneously features an intrusion of unusual combinations." — https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/uzzi/htm/papers/science-2013-uzzi-468-72.pdf (READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q2, Q4, Q7, C3
    limit:    Novelty is measured by which journals are cited together, impact by citations; main figure covers 1990–2000.

F11. Surprising combinations mostly come from researchers moving into a distant field. Papers from the most surprising of these moves are 3.5× likelier than random to be hits.
    who:      Feng Shi & James Evans (19.9M MEDLINE papers, physics journals, patents), Nat Commun 2023
    evidence: "Papers representing the most surprising publication expeditions are 3.5 times more likely than random to be hit papers." — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10039062/ (READ-PARTIAL)
    bears on: Q1, Q3, C3
    limit:    Correlation only; "hits" are defined by citations.

F12. Late recognition ("sleeping beauties") is common across 22M papers, and the papers that wake up are mostly cited from other disciplines.
    who:      Ke, Ferrara, Radicchi & Flammini, Indiana (PNAS 2015)
    evidence: "for about 80% of the top SBs, as much as 75% or more of citations are of interdisciplinary nature" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4475978/ (READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q2, Q3, C4
    limit:    The authors warn that comparing across disciplines or ages "may be problematic".

F13. Of 773,311 papers from 2001, 89% cite no new combination of journals. The most novel have 39.82% higher odds of reaching the top 1% by citations, and 62.37% higher odds of being highly cited in other fields. They are less likely to be top-cited within 3 years and appear in journals with lower Impact Factors.
    who:      Wang, Veugelers & Stephan (NBER w22180; later Research Policy 2017)
    evidence: "highly novel papers are less likely to be top cited when using citation time windows shorter than 3 years" — https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22180/w22180.pdf (READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q2, Q6, C4
    limit:    I read the working paper; the journal version's numbers may differ.

F14. Readers use author reputation to choose what to read. In an APA study, 15–23% of psychologists' choices of which article to read rested on who the authors were. Any one chemist reads about 0.5% of published chemistry articles.
    who:      Robert K. Merton (Science 1968), drawing on Zuckerman's interviews with Nobel laureates
    evidence: "scientists search for cues to what they should attend to" — https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/merton/matthew1.pdf (READ-FULL, poor OCR)
    bears on: Q2, C4
    limit:    Pre-digital; anecdotal apart from the APA figures.

F15. Across 17,154 citations reported by 9,380 authors, 54% had little or no influence on the citing author. The chance a citation marked major influence was 6.2% for a paper with 1 citation, 15.6% at 1,000 and 20.6% at 10,000. When the survey randomly showed citation counts, respondents rated less-cited papers lower on quality.
    who:      Teplitskiy, Duede, Menietti & Lakhani (Research Policy 2022)
    evidence: "Authors discover highly cited papers earlier in their projects, more often through social contacts, and read them more closely." — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.10033 (READ-FULL)
    bears on: Q1, Q2, C4
    limit:    About 15% of those invited completed the survey; answers are self-reported.

## Against the claims
A1 (C4). Popularity partly tracks quality. In MusicLab (14,341 participants, 48 songs), social influence made success more unequal and less predictable, but the extremes held. F15 also finds famous papers 2–3× likelier to be truly influential.
    who: Salganik, Dodds & Watts (Science 2006)
    evidence: "The best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible." — https://www.princeton.edu/~mjs3/salganik_dodds_watts06_full.pdf (READ-FULL)
    limit: Music, not research; participants recruited mostly from a teen website.

A2 (C3). New connections are rare and under-rewarded. In 6,455,756 MEDLINE abstracts, 85.8% of statements repeat known relationships and 1.8% jump to new ones. Mean citations: 8.38 for repeat work, 11.00 for new links, 12.90 for jumps.
    who: Foster, Rzhetsky & Evans (ASR 2015)
    evidence: "the additional reward is insufficient to compensate for the additional risk" — https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.6906 (READ-FULL)
    limit: Biomedical chemistry only.

A3 (C1 done by following links). Following links may lead everyone to the same prevailing opinion. Evans (34M articles): as journals went online, citations went to newer work and to fewer papers. This is contested.
    evidence: "following hyperlinks quickly puts researchers in touch with prevailing opinion, but this may accelerate consensus and narrow the range of findings and ideas built upon" — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18635800/ (abstract; body BLOCKED). Rebuttal (Larivière et al.): "contrary to what was reported by Evans, the dispersion of citations is actually increasing" — https://arxiv.org/pdf/0809.5250 (READ-PARTIAL)

A4 (C1/C2 in large fields). In large fields the same classic papers stay on top. Across 90M papers and 1.8B citations, the predicted share of disruptive papers falls 49% → 27% → 13% as a field publishes 1k → 10k → 100k papers a year.
    who: Chu & Evans (PNAS 2021)
    evidence: "cognitively overloaded reviewers and readers process new work only in relationship to existing exemplars" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8522281/ (READ-FULL)
    limit: Correlation only.

A5 (C2). Experts do not start broad; they go straight to known sources. In a 2×2 study of 24 people, only those strong in both web search and the subject solved 3.2 of 5 tasks; every other group solved 2 or fewer.
    who: Hölscher & Strube, Freiburg (WWW9 2000)
    evidence: "Only "double experts" initially tried to access directly web-sites related to economics, while all others immediately accessed a search engine" — https://web.archive.org/web/2003id_/http://www9.org/w9cdrom/81/81.html (READ-FULL)
    limit: Small sample, one subject area, web of 2000.

## Not covered by any claim
N1. Unpublished ("grey") literature changes the answer. Published trials show 15% larger effects than grey ones across 41 meta-analyses (ratio 1.15, 95% CI 1.04–1.28), and 9% larger in the Cochrane review. "published work, compared with grey literature, yielded significantly larger estimates of the intervention effect by 15%" — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11072941/ ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17443631/ (abstracts). Q3, Q5.
N2. Citing is not reading. Copied misprints in citations to one paper with 4,300 citations imply "only about 20% of citers read the original" — https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0212043 (READ-FULL; based on one paper). Q2.
N3. Stopping can be estimated statistically. A capture–recapture estimate found that 4 databases held 68% (title/abstract screening) and 81% (full-text screening) of the likely total: "Researchers have no empirically based search stopping rule" — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18722088/ . Swanson 1986: a search "can never be verified as capable of retrieving all information relevant to a problem or theory" (abstract via OpenAlex, doi 10.1086/601720). Q6.
