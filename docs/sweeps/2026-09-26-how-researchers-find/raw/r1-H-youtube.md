## Findings

F1. Hamming worked with his door open and ate lunch at the table of the strongest people: first the physicists (Bardeen, Shockley, Brattain), later the chemists. He noticed that people who worked with the door shut kept working on slightly the wrong problem.
    who: Richard Hamming, Bell Labs (Hamming codes). This was a 1995 course lecture, so it is performed advice.
    evidence: CAPTION-PARAPHRASE. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a1zDuOPkMSw&t=750 and &t=1103 (2026-09-26)
    bears on: Q1. None of the claims names learning from people rather than topics.
    limit: He said he could show only a correlation, not which way the cause runs.

F2. Hamming kept 10 to 20 open problems in mind. When a clue to attacking one of them turned up, he dropped everything else for it. He also set aside Friday afternoons (about 10% of his time) to ask what the important problems were.
    evidence: CAPTION-PARAPHRASE. …a1zDuOPkMSw&t=1936 and &t=1731
    bears on: Q4 (the list is the map everything new is matched against), C1

F3. Hamming "put hooks on ideas", a habit he copied from John Tukey. He turned each new piece of information around in many ways until it was tied to many other pieces he already had. He cut newspapers and magazines, and he took a book-review editor post to force himself to read widely. On a new field he decided fast: "interesting but not relevant, forget it".
    evidence: CAPTION-PARAPHRASE. …&t=2353, &t=1618, &t=1849
    bears on: C3, Q2, C2

F4. Ng's method for a new area: make a list of papers, skim about 10% of each, and drop the duds, including papers that other authors cite as wrong. Read the seminal papers in full, add new papers from their citations, then go back to the earlier ones. His rough stopping points: 15 to 20 papers for a basic grasp, 50 to 100 for a very good understanding.
    who: Andrew Ng, CS230 lecture from 2018 (288k views). He sells courses (Coursera, DeepLearning.AI).
    evidence: CAPTION-PARAPHRASE. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=733m6qBH-jI&t=211 and &t=328
    bears on: C1 and C2 (for), Q6

F5. Ng reads each part of a paper with the author's incentive in mind. The abstract, intro and conclusion are written to convince reviewers. The related-work section often cites likely reviewers to please them. Even a highly cited paper (LeNet-5) was half material that did not last. He reads 5 or 6 papers a week to choose 2 for his reading group. His sources are Twitter, r/MachineLearning ("a lot of noise"), conference title lists, and friends' Slack groups.
    evidence: CAPTION-PARAPHRASE. …&t=466, &t=649, &t=1325
    bears on: Q2, C4

F6. Cowen reads in clusters on one topic, and rereads a book only after 4 or 5 others on the same subject.
    who: Tyler Cowen, economist. EconTalk video, 9,750 views.
    evidence: "you learn methods, you read in clusters, you don't obsess over single books, you try to read on a project you're working on so you have context" (https://www.econtalk.org/tyler-cowen-on-reading/, 2026-09-26, READ-PARTIAL)
    bears on: C1, C3, Q4

F7. Cowen's quick "does it grab me" test comes with a condition about the era the book was written in.
    evidence: "older books are quite different. They were not written to grab people up front. So, if the author's bad at that, it's not a negative signal." (same page)
    bears on: Q2. A filter that depends on the kind of source.

F8. Collison uses Basho's line: follow what the people you admire admired, not the people themselves. He traces what came upstream of people doing great work, and says it is "often quite obscure". He opens a book in the middle and asks whether he would like to have ended up there. He drops the current book the moment a better one appears. He leaves physical books around the house so that a later mention can bring one back ("productive collisions").
    who: Patrick Collison, Stripe. Knowledge Project, 19k views.
    evidence: CAPTION-PARAPHRASE. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8MuNDps3m6A&t=4023, &t=4099, &t=4245
    bears on: Q1, Q3, C3

F9. Dwarkesh picks guests by whether he wants to spend 1 to 2 weeks reading everything they wrote, not by how famous they are.
    who: Dwarkesh Patel, podcaster.
    evidence: "big guests don't really matter that much if you just look at what are the most popular episodes … she, before I interviewed her, was just a scholar, who was not publicly well-known at all" (https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/scaling-ama; video …XLaRfZ4AHn8&t=967, READ-PARTIAL)
    bears on: C4 (for). View counts back it: the Paine episode has 5.21M views, Tao 498k.

F10. Dwarkesh describes a loop where good output gets him access to experts, and each field's experts lead to the next field.
    evidence: "that alone has netted me an amazing China network in the matter of one blog post … getting to meet people like you is actually the main sort of flywheel" (same page, &t=1443)
    bears on: Q1, Q4, C1

F11. Dwarkesh uses spaced repetition because what he learned earlier makes new learning faster ("learning compounds"). Before an interview he goes through the guest's writing with an LLM, looking for contradictions and blind spots to raise with them.
    evidence: CAPTION-PARAPHRASE. https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Vm49oIPtyRE (2024)
    limit: In 2026 he said: "able to move much faster on some things through the help of AI, but I don't know if I'm learning better" (https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/michael-nielsen)
    bears on: Q4, Q5

F12. Tao learns a new field through a person in it.
    who: Terence Tao, mathematician (Dwarkesh, 2026-03, 498k views).
    evidence: "I just make friends with another mathematician working on another area of mathematics … they have to teach me some of the basic tricks, what's known, and what's not known." (https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/terence-tao, …Q8Fkpi18QXU&t=4289, READ-PARTIAL)
    bears on: Q1, Q3 (what is *not* known lives in people's heads)

F13. Tao on the roughly 50 Erdős problems that AI solved: they had almost no literature, and the solution was a join of two existing things.
    evidence: "it was just combining this one obscure technique that not many people know about with some other result in the literature." (same page)
    bears on: C3 (for), Q3

F14. Gwern builds long syntheses from small notes kept over years.
    who: Gwern Branwen, independent writer who saw LLM scaling early. Lives on about $12k a year from Patreon.
    evidence: "If you could see all of the times I just note down something like, "Hmm, that's funny." Or, "Huh, another example of that,"" and "a bunch of disparate things that I have been making notes on that I don't even realize are connected. They just bother me for a long time." (https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/gwern-branwen; …a42key59cZQ&t=3766, &t=4044; section read whole)
    bears on: C1 and C3 (strong for), Q4

F15. Gwern on what the crowd and the papers miss. On the 2017 Baidu scaling paper: "It should have been the most important paper of the year, but a lot of people just did not prioritize it." On the literature itself: "Papers do not tell you where the ideas come from in a truthful manner." When GPT-3 came out, Twitter read it as showing that scaling worked badly.
    evidence: same page, &t=1045
    bears on: C4 (for), Q2

F16. Gwern's daily routine: first "clean up the previous day's work … think if I properly collated everything and put it in the right places", then Twitter and RSS. He uses arguing online as motivation and as a check on himself, because working alone you "could be arbitrarily wrong."
    evidence: same page, &t=4263
    bears on: Q4, Q5

F17. Paine checks a book's sources in 30 seconds: "Go flip to the bibliography, flip through it for 30 seconds, and see if at least some of the citations are in the languages of the countries being discussed." She reads Russian, Japanese and Chinese sources herself, "with lots of large dictionaries". She uses archives to find unknown unknowns. She warns against "half-court tennis": studying the world only from your own side.
    who: Sarah Paine, US Naval War College historian.
    evidence: https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/sarah-paine (…YcVSgYz5SJ8&t=2545, &t=2479, &t=2727; READ-PARTIAL)
    bears on: Q2, Q3, C5 (for)

F18. Paine on citation cascades: "everybody starts citing Edgar Snow, and then we cite everybody and everybody and everybody. But actually, it only goes back to Edgar Snow."
    evidence: https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/sarah-paine-china (…4l3Sa8ImGFQ&t=1207)
    bears on: C4, Q5. A citation count can hide the fact that there is only one real source.

    evidence: https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/noam-brown (…6AgOfiZOWiY&t=601, 2026-09-17)

## Against the claims

- **Against C5 (Ilya Sutskever):** "The top-down belief is the thing that sustains you when the experiments contradict you. Because if you trust the data all the time, well sometimes you can be doing the correct thing but there's a bug." Here, evidence against the idea is sometimes overridden on purpose. Source: …aR20FWCCjAs&t=5693.
- **Against C2 (Abbeel, CAPTION-PARAPHRASE):** Schulman spent about a year on RL for a single pendulum and read "pretty much all" RL papers. That is depth first, on a topic that "was in a side room nobody visited" at conferences. Abbeel also says TRPO is highly cited partly because it came first, and that the originators of an idea know its limits before the enthusiasts do. Source: …kr6k_SyKsh0&t=464, &t=754, &t=957 (6,029 views).
- **Nuance against C4 (Hamming):** because of the Matthew effect, fame bought him access to information, since IBM and others showed him their labs. Popularity partly feeds quality, not only the reverse. Source: …a1zDuOPkMSw&t=92.
